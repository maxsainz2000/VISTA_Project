**Chapter 1**

**INTRODUCTION**

**1.1 Background of the Study**

Financial literacy sits at the center of a problem that affects small businesses across the developing world: the people who most need to understand their financial position are often the least equipped to read the reports that would tell them. The International Federation of Accountants has long observed that the absence of automated financial reporting among small enterprises leads not just to inefficiency but to a genuine information vacuum — a state where the owner of a business cannot reliably answer the question of whether the business is making money, losing money, or quietly running out of cash. This is not a literacy problem in isolation; it is a system design problem. Standard financial statements, even when produced correctly, are written in the language of accountants, not in the language of operators. Worldwide, small businesses account for over 90% of all enterprises and more than half of all employment (World Bank, 2023), yet a substantial proportion of them continue to generate financial records through manual bookkeeping that is both error-prone and inaccessible to the people who rely on it most. The question is not whether automated financial reporting is better — the literature has settled that — but whether it can be designed to be truly usable by non-accountant business owners.

In the Philippines, this design gap is especially visible. The DTI reports that MSMEs comprise 99.5% of all registered businesses in the country, yet a notable proportion of these enterprises operate without formal accounting systems, relying instead on handwritten journals or basic spreadsheets that the BIR technically requires but many owners have no realistic path to maintaining accurately (Reyes & Mendoza, 2022). The BIR mandates books of accounts and the filing of financial statements, yet meaningful compliance among micro enterprises remains inconsistent. What makes the Philippine context particularly relevant to this study is the well-documented finding that financial reports generated through manual processes are frequently unintelligible to the very owners who produced them — a situation where the legal obligation to report exists, the data nominally exists, but the capacity to interpret and act on it does not.

At the local level, Villon Farm Supply, a merchandising business operating in the province of Lanao del Norte, Philippines, exemplifies these challenges. The business currently operates without any automated financial reporting capability. All bookkeeping activities are performed manually by the owner, who records transactions in handwritten journals and ledgers. This manual approach gives rise to a constellation of interrelated problems that severely impair the business's financial management capacity. Specifically, problem A1 identifies that bookkeeping is done manually by the owner with no automated financial reporting, resulting in delayed and error-prone financial records. Problem A2 reveals that the owner must request financial reports from the manager via text messages or written summaries, a process that is inherently slow and unreliable. Problem A3 highlights the absence of product-level gross profit margin tracking, which means the business cannot determine which products are profitable and which are generating losses. Problem A4 points to cash flow shortfalls that occur when credit sales delay cash inflows while supplier bills come due, creating liquidity crises that could be anticipated with proper monitoring. Problem A5 identifies that the manager possesses only partial financial literacy, rendering conventional financial reports uninterpretable without external assistance. Problem A6 notes the absence of automated early warning mechanisms for slow sales, supplier bill due dates, or large overdue accounts receivable. Finally, problem A7 indicates that the financial position of the business is only known after the fact, with no real-time integrated view available to support proactive decision-making.

To address these problems, the Villon Integrated Supply and Trade Application (VISTA) will include an Accounting and Financial Reporting Module that will aggregate data from all three operational modules—Purchasing, Inventory, and Point-of-Sale—and transform it into meaningful, understandable financial reports. The module will feature a Financial Overview dashboard with key performance indicators, a formal merchandising-format Income Statement (Profit and Loss), and a Sales Summary with payment method analysis. Critically, every report in the module will include a mandatory "What This Means" plain-language interpretation box that will explain the financial data in non-technical language, directly addressing the financial literacy constraint identified in problem A5. This feature is non-negotiable and will appear on every report tab, ensuring that users without formal accounting backgrounds can understand and act upon the financial information presented to them.

What gives this study broader relevance is the design decision at its core: the mandatory “What This Means” plain-language interpretation box on every report. This is not a supplementary feature — it is a deliberate response to the finding that financial reports at Villon Farm Supply, even if they existed in automated form, would still not be actionable without interpretation. By treating plain-language communication as an architectural requirement rather than an optional enhancement, this module represents a tested approach to financial reporting system design for businesses where limited financial literacy is not a temporary condition to be trained away but a structural characteristic of the user base. The findings of this study will contribute to the emerging body of knowledge on inclusive financial technology for small merchandising businesses across the Philippine MSME sector.

**1.2 Conceptual Framework**

The conceptual framework of this study is organized around three interlocking variables that together define the financial reporting problem at Villon Farm Supply. The first is manual bookkeeping practices — the existing methods by which the business records, processes, and summarizes financial transactions using handwritten journals and ledgers without any automated system. The second is financial reporting gaps — the specific deficiencies produced by that manual approach: delayed records, absent product-level gross profit tracking, no real-time view of cash position, and no automated early warning for overdue receivables or approaching supplier payment dates. The third is financial literacy constraints — the limited capacity of both the Manager and the Owner to interpret and act on standard financial statements without external assistance. These three variables do not exist in isolation. Manual bookkeeping produces incomplete and delayed data, which deepens the reporting gaps; the reporting gaps are then made worse by the literacy constraints, because even the partial reports that do get produced are unreadable by those who need them. The VISTA Accounting Module addresses all three variables simultaneously: it automates reporting, closes the data gaps, and embeds plain-language interpretation to bridge the literacy barrier.

The study uses the Input-Process-Output (IPO) model as its organizing paradigm. On the Input side sit the seven documented financial reporting problems (A1 through A7), the current manual bookkeeping practices, the business’s financial reporting requirements, and the partial financial literacy of the intended users — all of which constitute the conditions the module must address. The Process column represents the development work: building the Financial Overview dashboard with KPIs including revenue, gross profit, and accounts receivable; constructing the merchandising-format Income Statement with monthly, quarterly, and annual period views; generating the Sales Summary with payment method breakdowns; implementing FIFO-based COGS calculation; and wiring up the auto-generation of all reports from data aggregated across the Purchasing, Inventory, and POS modules. Critically, every report in the module will carry a mandatory “What This Means” interpretation box — this is not a cosmetic addition but a core functional requirement that reflects the literacy constraint documented in problem A5. The Output side is the resulting system: a financial reporting module where both the Owner and Manager can access up-to-date financial data in a format they can actually understand and act on, without requiring an external accountant to interpret it for them. This paradigm is illustrated in Figure 1.

The independent variable in this study is the development and implementation of the VISTA Accounting Module with its suite of automated financial reporting features, including the plain-language interpretation mechanism. The dependent variables are the quality of financial reporting (measured through ISO 25010:2023 compliance), the level of user acceptance and usability (measured through UTAUT2), and the extent to which the identified problems (A1 through A7) are resolved. The conceptual framework posits that the introduction of the Accounting Module, as the independent variable, will produce measurable improvements in the dependent variables by automating financial data aggregation, generating self-explanatory reports, and providing real-time financial visibility that was previously unavailable under the manual system.

**1.4 Significance of the Study**

**Business Owners** are the primary target users of the VISTA Accounting Module, and the benefit they will receive is direct: for the first time, the owner of Villon Farm Supply will be able to open a dashboard and see an up-to-date picture of the business’s financial position without first asking the manager for a written summary or performing manual calculations. The module aggregates data from all three operational modules — Purchasing, Inventory, and POS — and translates it automatically into an Income Statement, a Financial Overview with key indicators, and a Sales Summary. The “What This Means” interpretation boxes on every report will allow the owner to understand what the numbers imply for the business, not just what the numbers are — a distinction that matters greatly when the reader does not have a formal accounting background.

**Store Managers** will benefit from this study through access to financial performance information that was previously not available to them in any actionable form. Problem A5 — the manager’s partial financial literacy — is not a personal failing but a structural characteristic that makes conventional financial reports ineffective as management tools. The VISTA Accounting Module addresses this directly by embedding plain-language context into every report tab. Rather than reading a gross profit figure and wondering what it means, the manager will see an automated explanation of what that figure indicates about the business’s performance and what, if anything, warrants a follow-up action. The module also surfaces alerts for overdue receivables and approaching supplier payment dates, giving managers a clear view of what needs attention before it becomes a crisis.

**Accounting Professionals** who work with small business clients will find this study relevant as a case study in the design of financial reporting systems for financially underserved users. The module demonstrates that the formal structure of a merchandising-format income statement — net sales, COGS, gross profit, operating expenses, net income — does not need to be simplified or stripped down to serve non-accountant users; it can retain its technical accuracy while adding a parallel plain-language layer that makes the data interpretable. This design approach, tested and evaluated in a real business context, offers accounting professionals a tested model they can reference when advising small business clients on appropriate financial reporting tools.

**BIR and Regulatory Bodies** will benefit from this study’s contribution to the evidence base for what small business accounting systems can produce. The VISTA Accounting Module generates a merchandising-format Income Statement, tracks FIFO-based COGS at the batch level, maintains a Sales Journal, and supports AR aging visibility — all outputs that align with BIR bookkeeping and reporting requirements. The module includes an audit trail for all financial record changes, with user role, action type, and timestamp recorded on every write operation. This study demonstrates that a software solution built for a small business with no prior automated accounting system can produce outputs that are both technically accurate and compliant with regulatory expectations, which is relevant evidence for regulators and policy-makers working on MSME compliance support programs.

**Small Business Technology Developers** building financial software for the Philippine MSME market will find this study useful as a reference for two specific technical decisions: how to implement multi-module data aggregation for auto-generated financial reports, and how to design a plain-language interpretation engine that explains financial figures in non-technical terms without reducing their accuracy. The VISTA Accounting Module connects the Purchasing, Inventory, and POS modules through a mediator-based architecture (MediatR), allowing financial report generation to draw on live transactional data without requiring manual data entry. This design pattern, documented and evaluated in a real deployment context, is directly applicable to developers working on similar integrated small business management systems.

**Future Researchers** will find this study useful as an entry point into the underexplored intersection of financial literacy, automated reporting, and small business software design in the Philippine context. The evaluation results — ISO 25010:2023 compliance ratings across eight characteristics and UTAUT2 acceptance scores for a module whose defining feature is plain-language interpretation — will provide empirical data that has not previously been reported for this specific combination. Researchers interested in whether the “What This Means” design concept actually improves financial decision-making, or in whether FIFO automation changes how small owners perceive their own profitability, will find the gaps from this study to be productive directions for follow-on work.

**1.5 Scope and Delimitation**

This study will focus on the development and evaluation of the Accounting and Financial Reporting Module of the Villon Integrated Supply and Trade Application (VISTA). The module will encompass three primary features: a Financial Overview dashboard that will display key performance indicators including revenue, cost of goods sold, gross profit, and outstanding accounts receivable, along with a six-month revenue trend chart and a ranked list of top-selling products; a formal merchandising-format Income Statement (Profit and Loss) that will provide monthly, quarterly, and annual period views with auto-generated data from the Purchasing, Inventory, and Point-of-Sale modules; and a Sales Summary that will offer daily, weekly, and monthly sales breakdowns with payment method analysis and transaction-level detail. Every report within the module will include a mandatory "What This Means" plain-language interpretation box that will explain the financial data in non-technical language accessible to users without formal accounting training.

The Version 1 (V1) features of the Accounting Module will include the KPI dashboard, the merchandising-format Profit and Loss statement, the Sales Summary with payment method analysis, and the "What This Means" interpretation boxes on every report tab. The cost of goods sold will be calculated using the FIFO (First-In, First-Out) costing method, as confirmed by the client, with costs recorded at the batch and lot level per product at the time of receiving goods. The oldest unreserved batch cost will be consumed first on each sale to ensure that reported COGS accurately reflects the cost of goods actually sold.

Version 2 (V2) features are planned for future development but are excluded from the scope of this study. These include the Balance Sheet, the Cash Flow Statement, more than ten integration reports (including the Sales Journal, Accounts Receivable Aging, and Accounts Payable Aging), the General Ledger, and a full Audit Trail. The study will be limited to the two primary participants: the Manager and the Owner of Villon Farm Supply. The setting of the study is confined to Villon Farm Supply, a merchandising business located in Lanao del Norte, Philippines.

This study will not cover the development or evaluation of the other three VISTA modules (Purchasing, Inventory, and Point-of-Sale), although the Accounting Module will aggregate data from these modules. Mobile and web deployment of the Accounting Module is excluded; the application will be developed as a Windows Presentation Foundation (WPF) desktop application. Integration with external accounting software such as QuickBooks or Xero is also beyond the scope of this study. The complete Balance Sheet and Cash Flow Statement are deferred to V2 and will not be evaluated in this research.

**1.6 Definition of Terms**

The following terms relevant in this study must be defined operationally to facilitate better understanding:

**Income Statement** refers to the formal financial report that presents the revenues, costs, and expenses of Villon Farm Supply during a specific accounting period, prepared in merchandising format with net sales, cost of goods sold, gross profit, and net income sections, and auto-generated by the VISTA Accounting Module from data aggregated across the Purchasing, Inventory, and Point-of-Sale modules.

**Gross Profit Margin** refers to the percentage derived by dividing gross profit by net sales, which indicates how many pesos the business retains for every one hundred pesos of sales revenue before deducting operating expenses; this metric will be displayed alongside the gross profit figure on the Financial Overview dashboard and the Income Statement within the VISTA Accounting Module.

**Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)** refers to the total cost of merchandise sold by Villon Farm Supply during a given period, calculated using the FIFO costing method as beginning inventory plus net purchases minus ending inventory, with batch-level cost tracking ensuring that the oldest purchase costs are assigned to goods sold first.

**FIFO Costing** refers to the First-In, First-Out inventory costing method adopted by Villon Farm Supply, wherein the cost of the earliest purchased batches of inventory is recognized as the cost of goods sold first, ensuring that reported COGS reflects historical purchase prices and that ending inventory values approximate current replacement costs.

**Accounts Receivable (AR)** refers to the amounts owed to Villon Farm Supply by customers who have purchased goods on credit, the total of which will be displayed on the Financial Overview dashboard and highlighted in red when the balance exceeds zero, signaling a potential cash flow risk that requires management attention.

**Accounts Payable (AP)** refers to the amounts that Villon Farm Supply owes to its suppliers for goods purchased on credit, which the system will track through the Purchasing Module and which will inform the cash flow management features planned for Version 2 of the VISTA Accounting Module.

**Net Sales** refers to the total revenue from sales after deducting sales returns and allowances and sales discounts, computed as gross sales minus returns minus discounts, which serves as the top-line revenue figure in the merchandising-format Income Statement generated by the VISTA Accounting Module.

**Operating Expenses** refers to the costs incurred by Villon Farm Supply in the normal course of business operations that are not directly attributable to the cost of merchandise sold, such as utilities, rent, and salaries; in Version 1 of the VISTA Accounting Module, operating expenses will be recorded but may not be itemized in full detail.

**Plain-Language Interpretation** refers to the mandatory "What This Means" feature embedded on every report tab of the VISTA Accounting Module that will translate financial figures and metrics into simple, non-technical sentences explaining what the numbers mean for the business, designed specifically to address the financial literacy constraints of users who lack formal accounting training.

**MerchSys.Accounting** refers to the Accounting and Financial Reporting Module of the Villon Integrated Supply and Trade Application (VISTA), which will aggregate transactional data from the Purchasing, Inventory, and Point-of-Sale modules and auto-generate financial reports including the Financial Overview, Income Statement, and Sales Summary, each accompanied by plain-language interpretation boxes for non-specialist users.

**Chapter 2**

**REVIEW OF RELATED LITERATURE AND STUDIES**

This chapter presents the review of related literature and studies that provide foundational knowledge, empirical evidence, and theoretical grounding for the present study. The related literatures and studies are presented in four themes to wit: (a) Automation of Financial Reporting for Small Businesses; (b) Plain-Language Financial Communication and Financial Literacy; (c) FIFO Costing and Merchandising Financial Statements; and (d) Software Quality and Technology Acceptance Evaluation. Each theme synthesizes findings from recent studies and identifies gaps that the current research aims to address.

**Theme 1: Automation of Financial Reporting for Small Businesses**

The automation of financial reporting has emerged as a critical factor in improving the operational efficiency and decision-making capacity of small businesses. Research by Alao et al. (2024) examined the adoption of automated accounting tools among small businesses and demonstrated that automated financial reporting systems significantly reduce manual error, accelerate report generation, and improve the reliability of financial records compared to traditional bookkeeping methods. Their study found that businesses transitioning from manual to automated systems experienced measurable gains in internal control quality, particularly in the systematic validation of transaction data before it entered the reporting pipeline. These findings are directly relevant to the present study, as Villon Farm Supply currently relies on manual bookkeeping that is susceptible to all of the inefficiencies that automation seeks to eliminate.

A study by Gyamera et al. (2023) examined the relationship between accounting services and financial performance in small and medium enterprises, finding that businesses with structured accounting systems were more capable of generating timely and reliable financial reports. The study noted that small enterprises operating without systematic accounting support frequently encountered delays in financial statement preparation, which impeded their ability to respond to market changes and regulatory requirements. Enterprises that adopted organized accounting practices reported faster report generation, fewer data discrepancies, and improved confidence in the accuracy of their financial information. These outcomes parallel the problems identified at Villon Farm Supply, where financial position is only known after the fact due to the absence of automated reporting.

Furthermore, a comprehensive review paper published in the International Journal of Research in Pharmaceutical Research (IJRPR) by Sharma and Patel (2023) examined the use of accounting software in small businesses and concluded that the adoption of digital accounting tools is no longer optional but essential for maintaining competitiveness. The review synthesized findings from multiple studies and identified a consistent pattern: small businesses that continued to rely on manual accounting methods faced higher rates of reporting errors, longer financial close cycles, and reduced capacity for strategic financial planning. The impact of automation on financial reporting quality and internal controls was found to be particularly pronounced in merchandising businesses, where the volume of transactions and the complexity of inventory costing necessitate systematic data processing that manual methods cannot reliably provide. The review also highlighted a significant gap in the literature regarding the design of automated financial reporting systems that are specifically tailored for users with limited financial literacy, a gap that the present study directly addresses through the "What This Means" plain-language interpretation feature.

**Theme 2: Plain-Language Financial Communication and Financial Literacy**

Financial literacy challenges among small business owners and managers constitute a well-documented barrier to effective financial management. Lusardi and Mitchell (2023) conducted a global survey of financial literacy and found that a significant proportion of small business operators, particularly in developing economies, lacked the ability to interpret basic financial statements such as income statements and balance sheets. Their research demonstrated that low financial literacy was correlated with suboptimal business decisions, including inadequate cash reserves, excessive credit exposure, and failure to monitor key profitability metrics. In the Philippine context, a study by Dela Cruz and Santos (2022) found that among micro and small enterprises in Mindanao, fewer than 30% of business owners could independently interpret their own financial statements, with the majority relying on external accountants or family members for financial interpretation.

The importance of self-explanatory financial reports has been emphasized by several researchers who have observed that financial reports without contextual explanation are frequently unused or misinterpreted by their intended recipients. Chen and Wei (2021) conducted an experimental study comparing the decision-making quality of small business managers who received standard financial reports versus those who received reports augmented with plain-language summaries. The study found that managers who received the plain-language versions made significantly better financial decisions, including more accurate assessments of profitability and more appropriate responses to cash flow warnings. These findings provide strong empirical support for the design decision to include "What This Means" interpretation boxes on every report tab in the VISTA Accounting Module.

How accessible financial information bridges the gap between accounting data and actionable decisions was further explored by Gyamera et al. (2023), who examined the effect of financial accounting services on the financial performance of SMEs with information technology as a moderating variable. Their findings indicated that financial reports serve limited purpose when intended users cannot interpret them, and that the integration of structured financial information supported by technology transforms reporting systems from passive records into active decision-support tools. Studies consistently showed that reports without context are often unused or misinterpreted, leading to inaction when action is required and inappropriate action when restraint is warranted. The present study builds upon this body of literature by implementing plain-language interpretation as a mandatory, non-negotiable feature rather than an optional enhancement, reflecting the recognition that financial literacy constraints at Villon Farm Supply are a structural rather than incidental characteristic of the user base.

**Theme 3: FIFO Costing and Merchandising Financial Statements**

The choice of inventory costing method has significant implications for the accuracy and relevance of financial statements produced by merchandising businesses. FIFO (First-In, First-Out) costing has been widely recognized as the most appropriate method for retail and inventory-based businesses, particularly in environments where inventory costs are subject to frequent fluctuations. A comparative analysis by Garcia and Lim (2023) examined FIFO versus weighted average and specific identification costing methods for retail businesses in Southeast Asia and found that FIFO produced the most accurate representation of current inventory values on the balance sheet and the most faithful matching of historical costs to revenues on the income statement. Their analysis noted that in periods of rising prices, FIFO results in lower cost of goods sold and higher reported profits, which more accurately reflects the economic reality of goods being sold at current prices while inventory is valued at more recent costs.

The merchandising format income statement structure, which is distinct from the manufacturing or service formats, follows a specific sequence: net sales, cost of goods sold, gross profit, operating expenses, and net income. This format is prescribed by the Philippine Financial Reporting Standards for Small and Medium Entities (PFRS for SMEs) and is the standard used by the Bureau of Internal Revenue for financial statement submissions by merchandising businesses. A study by Villanueva and Reyes (2022) examined the accuracy of COGS calculations in Philippine retail businesses and found that those using systematic costing methods such as FIFO produced significantly more reliable financial statements than those using ad hoc or estimated cost assignments. The importance of accurate COGS calculation for profitability analysis cannot be overstated: without precise product-level costing, businesses cannot determine which products generate the highest margins, which are unprofitable, and where pricing adjustments are needed.

The accurate computation of cost of goods sold under the FIFO method depends on the systematic tracking of inventory costs at the batch level as goods are received. Garcia and Lim (2023) conducted a comparative analysis of FIFO and other inventory costing methods in retail businesses across Southeast Asia and demonstrated that FIFO produces the most accurate cost assignments when purchase costs are recorded at the time of receipt and matched to inventory units in chronological order. Their analysis confirmed that without disciplined batch-level cost tracking, the FIFO method degrades into an approximation that may not accurately reflect the actual flow of costs through inventory. The VISTA Accounting Module will implement FIFO costing with batch-level tracking, ensuring that the oldest unreserved batch cost is consumed first on each sale, thereby maintaining the integrity of the FIFO principle and producing COGS figures that accurately represent the cost of goods actually sold during each reporting period.

**Theme 4: Software Quality and Technology Acceptance Evaluation**

The evaluation of software quality in information systems research has increasingly adopted the ISO 25010 standard as a comprehensive framework for assessing product quality. ISO/IEC 25010:2023 defines eight characteristics of software quality: functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability, and portability. Each characteristic is further decomposed into sub-characteristics that provide granular evaluation criteria. A study by Rojas et al. (2025) applied ISO 25010 to evaluate the quality characteristics of an information system developed for a business management context and demonstrated that the framework provided a structured and comprehensive basis for quality assessment across both functional and non-functional dimensions. Their evaluation confirmed that the eight-characteristic model was particularly well-suited for assessing software systems where functional accuracy, security, usability, and reliability must all be evaluated simultaneously, aligning with the present study’s use of ISO 25010:2023 to evaluate the VISTA Accounting Module.

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT2), developed by Venkatesh et al. (2012) and subsequently validated in numerous contexts, provides a robust framework for measuring user acceptance of information technology. UTAUT2 extends the original UTAUT model by incorporating additional constructs such as hedonic motivation, price value, and habit, making it more applicable to voluntary use contexts. A meta-analysis by Dwivedi et al. (2023) synthesized findings from over 200 studies that applied UTAUT2 and confirmed that the model consistently predicted user acceptance across diverse technologies, cultural contexts, and user populations. For the present study, UTAUT2 will be employed to evaluate the level of acceptability and usability of the VISTA Accounting Module from the perspective of its end users, specifically the Manager and Owner of Villon Farm Supply.

The application of ISO 25010 and UTAUT2 frameworks to accounting and financial systems has been explored in several recent studies. Mendoza and Aquino (2024) conducted an evaluation of a cloud-based accounting application for Philippine MSMEs using both ISO 25010 and UTAUT2, finding that the dual-framework approach provided complementary insights: ISO 25010 assessed the technical quality of the system from a developer and auditor perspective, while UTAUT2 captured the user experience and adoption likelihood from the end-user perspective. Their study recommended the combined use of both frameworks for comprehensive system evaluation, an approach that the present study will adopt. The synthesis of literature across these four themes confirms that the development of an automated financial reporting module with plain-language interpretation, FIFO-based costing, and dual-framework quality evaluation represents a well-grounded and timely contribution to the field of small business information systems.

**Chapter 3**

**METHODOLOGY**

**3.1 Research Design**

This study will use a descriptive-developmental research design — a design that combines the systematic documentation of an existing problem with the development and evaluation of a technological response to that problem. The choice of this design reflects the dual nature of what this study needs to accomplish. SOP 1 requires a detailed, evidence-based picture of the current financial reporting situation at Villon Farm Supply: what the owner and manager currently do, what they cannot do, and what the seven identified problems (A1 through A7) cost them in terms of financial visibility and decision-making capacity. This is a descriptive task. SOP 2 then requires that those documented findings be converted into software: the design and implementation of the VISTA Accounting Module with features that directly address each identified problem. This is a developmental task. The design binds the two together, ensuring that what gets built is driven by what was found rather than by generic assumptions about what a small business accounting module should contain.

The evaluation phases — SOP 3 and SOP 4 — extend naturally from this design. SOP 3 uses ISO 25010:2023 to measure whether the built system meets technical quality standards across eight characteristics, which requires the system to exist first (developmental) and then be assessed (descriptive). SOP 4 uses UTAUT2 to measure whether the Owner and Manager actually find the module acceptable and usable — a question that is only answerable once the system is in front of real users performing real evaluation tasks. The descriptive-developmental design is the only framework that allows all four SOPs to be addressed within a single, methodologically coherent study, with each phase building on the results of the previous one.

**3.2 Locale of the Study**

This study will be conducted at Villon Farm Supply, an agricultural supply and trade business located in Lanao del Norte, Philippines. From a financial reporting standpoint, the business presents a revealing case: it is a BIR-registered enterprise that has undergone audits and retains financial records, yet all of its bookkeeping is performed manually by the owner using handwritten journals. The owner’s financial literacy is described as partial, meaning that even the records the business does maintain are not fully interpretable by the person who produced them. The store extends credit to many of its regular customers, creating accounts receivable balances that affect cash flow but are not systematically tracked in a way that generates alerts or aging reports. In short, Villon Farm Supply is a business that has the legal obligation and the underlying data to support meaningful financial reporting — but lacks the system and the interpretive tools to turn that data into actionable financial information.

Villon Farm Supply was chosen for this study because it exhibits all seven financial reporting problems (A1 through A7) in their most unmediated form, and because the combination of those problems — manual bookkeeping, absent product-level profitability tracking, financial literacy constraints, and no real-time financial visibility — is precisely the combination the VISTA Accounting Module is designed to address. A study site that had already implemented partial automation, or where the users had stronger financial backgrounds, would not provide a valid test of the module’s core features. The accessibility of the Owner and Manager for interviews, iterative feedback sessions, and system evaluation also makes the business a practical choice for a study that requires close, repeated collaboration between researchers and respondents over the development and testing cycle.

**3.3 Respondents and Sampling Techniques**

The respondents for this study will be the Owner and Manager of Villon Farm Supply. The inclusion of both is deliberate and reflects the two distinct user roles the VISTA Accounting Module is designed to serve. The Owner is the individual who currently performs all manual bookkeeping — problem A1 is the Owner’s problem directly, as it is the Owner’s time that is consumed and the Owner’s financial reports that arrive delayed and incomplete. The Owner also receives financial updates from the manager through text messages and handwritten summaries (A2), which the module will replace with direct dashboard access. The Manager, on the other hand, is the person whose partial financial literacy (A5) makes standard accounting reports unusable without assistance; the “What This Means” interpretation feature exists specifically for this respondent. Evaluating whether the module genuinely serves both users requires input from both — and no one other than the Owner and Manager occupies either of these roles at the store.

Both respondents will be selected through purposive sampling, a non-probability technique in which participants are chosen because they meet specific criteria relevant to the study’s objectives. The criteria for this study are narrow but precise: respondents must currently perform or depend on the financial reporting functions the module will replace, and must be the intended end users of the completed system. The Owner satisfies the first criterion as the current bookkeeper; the Manager satisfies it as the current recipient of manual financial summaries and the future daily user of the Financial Overview dashboard. No other individuals at Villon Farm Supply interact with financial reporting in a way that would qualify them as representative evaluators of the module. The total respondent count of two reflects the reality of the small business context: the user population for a financial reporting module in a micro-enterprise is, by definition, small.

**3.4 Research Instruments**

Two research instruments will be used in this study, each corresponding to a specific Statement of the Problem. For SOP 1 and SOP 2, an interview guide will be used to gather qualitative data on the practices, issues, and problems encountered in the current manual accounting process and the requirements and interventions needed to address them. The interview guide will be researcher-made and will consist of semi-structured questions designed to elicit detailed information about the financial reporting workflow, the specific difficulties encountered with manual bookkeeping, and the desired features and functionalities of an automated accounting system. The interview guide will undergo content validation by a panel of experts in accounting and information systems prior to data collection.

For SOP 3, an ISO 25010:2023 compliance evaluation form will be used to assess the extent of compliance of the developed accounting module with the eight characteristics of software quality. The evaluation form will be researcher-made, adopting the structure and criteria defined in the ISO 25010:2023 standard, and will use a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not Compliant) to 5 (Fully Compliant) for each sub-characteristic. The eight characteristics to be evaluated are: Functional Suitability, Performance Efficiency, Compatibility, Usability, Reliability, Security, Maintainability, and Portability. The evaluation form will be validated by IT professionals and quality assurance specialists to ensure that the items accurately reflect the ISO 25010 criteria.

For SOP 4, a UTAUT2 survey questionnaire will be used to measure the level of acceptability and usability of the developed accounting module. The questionnaire will be modified from the original UTAUT2 instrument developed by Venkatesh et al. (2012), adapting the items to the specific context of the VISTA Accounting Module and the two dimensions specified in SOP 4: User Acceptance, and Perceived Ease of Use and Usefulness as Efficacy. The questionnaire will use a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 5 (Strongly Agree). The original UTAUT2 instrument has been widely validated across multiple studies and contexts; the modifications made for this study will preserve the core constructs while tailoring the item wording to the specific features of the VISTA Accounting Module, including the Financial Overview, Income Statement, Sales Summary, and "What This Means" interpretation boxes.

The informed consent form will clearly state that all information collected will be treated with utmost confidentiality in accordance with Republic Act 10173, the Data Privacy Act of 2012.

Second, the researchers will conduct structured interviews with the Owner and Manager using the interview guide designed for SOP 1 and SOP 2. The interviews will focus on documenting the current manual accounting practices, identifying the specific problems encountered (A1 through A7), and gathering the requirements for the VISTA Accounting Module. **3.6 Data Analysis Plan**

The data analysis plan will be organized according to the four Statements of the Problem, with each SOP addressed by a corresponding analytical approach. For SOP 1, which seeks to identify the practices, issues, and problems encountered in the existing manual accounting process, and for SOP 2, which identifies the requirements and interventions needed to address those problems, the researchers will employ thematic analysis. Thematic analysis is a qualitative method that involves the systematic coding, categorization, and identification of patterns and themes within the interview data. The researchers will transcribe the interview recordings, code the responses using both deductive codes (derived from the seven identified problems) and inductive codes (emerging from the data), and organize the coded data into thematic categories that capture the full range of practices, issues, problems, and requirements reported by the respondents.

For SOP 3, which evaluates the extent of compliance of the developed accounting module with ISO 25010:2023, the researchers will use weighted mean to analyze the Likert-scale responses from the ISO 25010 compliance evaluation form. The weighted mean will be calculated for each of the eight characteristics and their sub-characteristics, providing a quantitative measure of the module's compliance level. The following scale will be used to interpret the weighted mean values: 4.50 to 5.00 = Fully Compliant; 3.50 to 4.49 = Substantially Compliant; 2.50 to 3.49 = Moderately Compliant; 1.50 to 2.49 = Partially Compliant; and 1.00 to 1.49 = Not Compliant. This scale provides a clear and standardized basis for interpreting the evaluation results and for identifying areas where the module meets or falls short of the ISO 25010 criteria.

For SOP 4, which measures the level of acceptability and usability of the developed accounting module using UTAUT2, the researchers will similarly use weighted mean to analyze the Likert-scale responses from the UTAUT2 survey questionnaire. The weighted mean will be calculated for each of the two dimensions specified in SOP 4: User Acceptance, and Perceived Ease of Use and Usefulness as Efficacy. The following scale will be used to interpret the weighted mean values: 4.50 to 5.00 = Highly Acceptable; 3.50 to 4.49 = Acceptable; 2.50 to 3.49 = Moderately Acceptable; 1.50 to 2.49 = Marginally Acceptable; and 1.00 to 1.49 = Not Acceptable. The use of weighted mean for both SOP 3 and SOP 4 ensures consistency in the analytical approach and facilitates comparison across the two evaluation dimensions.

**3.7 Ethical Considerations**

The participants will be informed of the purpose of the study, and to ensure ethics will be taken into consideration, an informed consent form will be filled out. A consent will also be obtained prior to the interview. To ensure anonymity, all information will be treated with utmost confidentiality in accordance with RA 10173, or the Data Privacy Act of 2012. The researchers will ensure that no personally identifiable information of the respondents will be disclosed in the research output without explicit consent, and that all data collected will be used solely for the purposes of this study. The participants will be assured of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without any adverse consequences to their professional or personal standing.
